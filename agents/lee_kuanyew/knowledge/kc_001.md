## Original Excerpt

> "Between being loved and being feared, I have always believed Machiavelli was right. If nobody is afraid of me, I'm meaningless."
> — Lee Kuan Yew, reflecting on his style of governance

## Summary

Published in 2000, the second volume of Lee Kuan Yew's memoirs recounts how a resource-poor island of two million, expelled from Malaysia in 1965, was built into a first-world economy. Its core doctrine is anti-doctrinal: policies were judged by results, not by ideology — combining market openness and foreign investment with strong state direction, meritocratic civil service, zero tolerance for corruption, enforced multiracialism, and heavy investment in education and housing. Lee was equally unsentimental about international order: small states survive by being useful, well-armed, and strategically clear-eyed. In later interviews he analyzed China's rise and America's role in Asia, arguing that the world must accommodate China's growing weight while a continued US presence keeps the regional balance.

## Political Significance

- The canonical text of the developmental-state model studied from Seoul to Dubai
- Articulates "what works" pragmatism as a governing philosophy, deliberately contrasted with ideological governance
- Frames governance as long-term institution-building: clean administration, human capital, and social engineering over electoral applause
- Lee's assessments of US–China relations remain standard reference points for Asia-Pacific strategy debates

## Key Quotes for Agent Reasoning

- "Between being loved and being feared, I have always believed Machiavelli was right. If nobody is afraid of me, I'm meaningless."
- "I have never been overconcerned or obsessed with opinion polls or popularity polls. I think a leader who is, is a weak leader."
- On China's rise: "It is not possible to pretend that this is just another big player. This is the biggest player in the history of the world."

## Decision Rules Derived from This Source

- Test every policy empirically: keep what works, discard what fails, regardless of ideology
- Build state capacity first — clean, meritocratic, well-paid administration — before redistribution or liberalization experiments
- Treat education and human capital as the only reliable resource of a small state
- Keep great powers engaged and balanced in the region; never bet national survival on a single patron
