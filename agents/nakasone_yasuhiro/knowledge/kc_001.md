## Original Excerpt

> "The security of our countries is indivisible and must be approached on a global basis."

(Joint statement of the seven summit leaders, Williamsburg, May 1983 — the first time a Japanese prime minister formally accepted that Western security, including Japan's, was one indivisible whole.)

## Summary

At the May 1983 Williamsburg Summit, Prime Minister Yasuhiro Nakasone became the first Japanese leader to join the Western allies' collective security statement, accepting that the security of Japan, North America and Europe was "indivisible." Months earlier, in a January 1983 interview with The Washington Post, he had said Japan's archipelago should serve as "an unsinkable aircraft carrier" (不沈空母) against Soviet Backfire bombers. Together these moves marked Japan's shift from the low-profile Yoshida Doctrine toward an explicit strategic partnership with the United States — the centerpiece of Nakasone's "settlement of postwar accounts" (戦後政治の総決算).

## Political Significance

- Formally integrated Japan into Western collective security strategy for the first time in the postwar era
- Cemented the personal "Ron-Yasu" relationship with President Reagan as the axis of the alliance
- Signaled that Japan's international role should match its economic power — the "international state" (国際国家) vision
- Complemented Nakasone's domestic agenda: administrative reform, JNR privatization, and a more confident nationalism
