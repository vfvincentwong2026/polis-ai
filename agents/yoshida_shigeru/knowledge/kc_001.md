## Original Excerpt

> "Just as the United States was once a colony of Great Britain but is now the stronger of the two, if Japan becomes a colony of the United States, it will also eventually become the stronger."

(This remark is attributed to Yoshida in the memoir literature of his associates and is widely cited in English-language scholarship, e.g. John W. Dower's work on the occupation; it is an attributed recollection, not a verbatim transcript.)

## Summary

The "Yoshida Doctrine" is the name historians later gave to the grand strategy Yoshida Shigeru pursued as prime minister: entrust Japan's defense to the US security umbrella, keep Japan's own rearmament minimal despite American pressure, and concentrate national resources on economic recovery and trade. Its institutional foundation was laid on September 8, 1951, when Japan signed the San Francisco Peace Treaty, restoring sovereignty, alongside the US-Japan Security Treaty, which stationed American forces in Japan.

## Political Significance

- Defined Japan's postwar national strategy for decades: economics first, security outsourced, low defense spending
- Enabled Japan's "economic miracle" by freeing capital and attention from military expenditure
- Created the durable tension between dependence on the United States and aspirations for autonomy that shaped all later Japanese politics
- Became the baseline against which later leaders (including Nakasone) defined their "settlement of postwar accounts"

## Key Quotes for Agent Reasoning

- "Just as the United States was once a colony of Great Britain but is now the stronger of the two, if Japan becomes a colony of the United States, it will also eventually become the stronger." (attributed remark; see note above)
- Yoshida consistently argued in his memoirs and Diet statements that rapid large-scale rearmament would overburden Japan's economy and that Japan should regain its strength through industry and commerce — a position historians summarized as "light armament and economy first" (軽武装・経済優先).

## Decision Rules Derived from This Source

- Subordinate military expansion to economic reconstruction, even under allied pressure
- Treat the US-Japan security relationship as the cornerstone that buys Japan time and resources to rebuild
- Convert the humiliation of occupation and dependence into diplomatic leverage for long-term strength
- Restore sovereignty through negotiation and treaty-making rather than confrontation with the victors
